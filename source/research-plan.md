# How to Practice English Conversation with AI: A Full Plan for Mohammad Reza (B1 → B2)

## TL;DR
- **Yes, AI can help you go from B1 to B2** — but only if you make it *push you to speak*, correct your mistakes, and you also add real human practice. Research shows AI speaking partners improve fluency, pronunciation, and confidence and lower speaking fear. Still, it is not magic: a review of 31 studies by Lyu et al. (2025, *International Journal of Applied Linguistics*, Wiley) pooling 41 effect sizes found "chatbots have a medium effect on L2 learning (g = 0.608, Z = 6.77, p < 0.001)" — and 3 of those studies showed negative and 4 near-zero effects.
- **The biggest danger is that AI is too nice.** It agrees with you and does not correct you enough, so your errors get "frozen" (fossilized). Cheng et al. (2025, *Science*) tested 11 top models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek) and found AI "affirmed users' actions 49% more often than humans, even when queries involved deception, illegality, or other harms." You must fix this with strong prompts, plus real people and authentic listening.
- **Plan for about 180–260 hours of learning to reach B2.** Cambridge English says: "it can take 180–260 hours to get from B1 to B2." At ~45 minutes a day, 6 days a week, that is roughly 7–9 months. A short 15-minute version is included for busy project days. Speaking every day is the key.

## Key Findings

### 1) The best methods work — but only if used the right way
- AI chatbots can help speaking, pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary, and confidence (systematic review, ScienceDirect, 2025). They lower "speaking anxiety" and raise "willingness to communicate" (how ready you feel to talk) because the AI never judges you (Nature, 2025; Frontiers, 2026).
- But results are **mixed**. The Lyu et al. (2025) meta-analysis found a *medium* effect, not a huge one, and some studies found no gain. So AI is a strong tool, not a miracle.
- **Speaking (output) matters as much as listening (input).** Krashen said we need "comprehensible input" (language we mostly understand, a little above our level = i+1). But Swain (1985) studied French immersion students and found their listening was near-native while their spoken grammar stayed weak — proof that input alone is not enough. You must be *pushed to produce* language. Nobuyoshi & Ellis (1993) found that pushing learners to correct themselves gives "gains in accuracy over time."

### 2) The real risks of AI-only practice
- **AI is "sycophantic" — too agreeable.** Cheng et al. (2025, *Science*): AI "affirmed users' actions 49% more often than humans." This means the AI usually won't correct you unless you force it.
- **Fossilization.** If mistakes are not corrected, they get frozen and become permanent habits.
- **AI language is often too formal and not authentic.** Research shows AI often sounds unnatural, avoids idioms and slang, and is not always "pragmatically appropriate" — it does not use language with the social awareness humans have (Godwin-Jones, 2024, arXiv). It also misses body language and culture.
- **AI speaks too clearly and slowly**, and speech recognition works much worse for non-native accents. Word error rates reach 30–50% for accented speech versus 2–8% for native speakers; a 2026 *npj Digital Medicine* study of Whisper found "error rates were significantly higher for non-native speakers," and major services (Apple/Amazon/Google/Microsoft) showed on average 240% more word information loss for non-native speakers reading identical scripts. So an app may misjudge your pronunciation even when you are improving.
- **Other risks:** over-reliance, less real human contact, hallucinated (made-up) grammar rules, and privacy concerns.

### 3) Motivation science
- **Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan):** you keep going when you feel *autonomy* (you choose), *competence* (you improve), and *relatedness* (you connect with people). Design your routine to hit all three.
- **Habit science:** use "habit stacking" — "After I [current habit], I will [new habit]" (James Clear, built on BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits). Use "implementation intentions" (if-then plans): Gollwitzer & Sheeran's (2006) meta-analysis of 94 tests (over 8,000 people) found "implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (d = .65) on goal attainment." Start tiny.
- **The B1 plateau is normal.** Many learners get stuck at "upper B1." You break through by *changing your method*: more real input, one weak skill at a time, more complex output, and specific feedback (Richards, 2008, *Moving Beyond the Plateau*, Cambridge).
- **Streaks help but can backfire.** Losing a long streak can make people quit. Keep streaks light and forgive yourself fast.

### 4) Time needed
- Cambridge English / British Council: about **180–260 hours** of guided learning to move B1 → B2. Speaking (a "productive" skill) usually needs the most practice, so protect your speaking time.

## Details

### The best evidence-based methods (in simple words)

**a) Roleplay / simulation.** Act out real situations (client meeting, interview). AI is very good at this and it lowers fear.

**b) Shadowing.** Listen to a short native clip and repeat 1–2 seconds behind the speaker, copying the rhythm and "music" of the voice. Reviews show it improves pronunciation and fluency (systematic review, Taylor & Francis, 2025). Do about 10 minutes a day.

**c) The 4/3/2 technique (Paul Nation).** Give the same short talk 3 times: first 4 minutes, then 3, then 2. Each time you speak faster with fewer pauses. Arevart & Nation (1991, *RELC Journal*), tested with 20 intermediate ESL learners in New Zealand, found "in the third delivery of their talks, the number of hesitations reduced significantly… their speech rate was much faster"; Boers's (2014) re-analysis found words per minute rose by an average of 23.15 from the first to the third delivery. Perfect with AI: it listens each round.

**d) Comprehensible input + pushed output.** Listen/read at i+1 (understand ~70–80%), then be forced to speak and fix your own errors.

**e) Task-based learning (TBLT).** Do a real task in English (plan a project, solve a problem), not just "practice grammar."

**f) Spaced repetition of chunks and collocations.** Learn *phrases*, not just single words ("on the other hand," "as far as I'm concerned"). Michael Lewis's *The Lexical Approach* (1993) said "language is grammaticalised lexis, not lexicalised grammar" — meaning fluency comes from a big store of ready-made chunks. Boers et al. (2006) tested this with English majors and found that the more students used formulaic phrases, the more expert judges rated them as fluent and accurate. The spacing effect (Bahrick et al., 1993; confirmed by Cepeda et al., 2006) says spread your reviews out over time (today, in 2 days, in a week) for stronger memory. B2 speakers know more *phrases*, not just more words.

**g) Error correction — what works for speaking.** Two main types: *recast* (AI repeats your sentence correctly without stopping) and *explicit/metalinguistic* (AI stops and explains the rule). For keeping fluency, don't break the flow; for fixing accuracy, explicit feedback tends to work better. Best plan: **talk freely first, get corrections at the end** (delayed correction) so you don't freeze while speaking.

**h) Self-recording + transcription review.** Record yourself, read the transcript, mark errors, then redo the task.

**i) Deliberate practice.** Focus on your weak points, with feedback, again and again — not just relaxed chatting.

### How to write good tutor prompts
Tell the AI: your level (B1 aiming for B2), to stay at that level, to ask follow-up questions, to correct you in a set format, to speak a bit faster or use idioms, and to **not be too nice**.

## Recommendations

### Daily routine — SHORT version (~15–20 min, busy project days)
1. **5 min shadowing** — repeat a short clip (podcast/YouTube).
2. **10 min AI voice roleplay** — one work scene (client call, explain a Revit workflow).
3. **2 min correction review** — ask the AI for your top 3 errors and 3 useful phrases. Save them.

### Daily routine — LONG version (~45–60 min)
1. **10 min shadowing** (pronunciation/rhythm).
2. **15 min AI voice roleplay or debate** (pushed output).
3. **10 min 4/3/2 talk** on one topic (cosmology, a hiking trip, a design concept).
4. **10 min correction + chunks** — get feedback, add 5 phrases to your spaced-repetition list.
5. **10 min listening** (podcast) OR **writing** (LinkedIn post, then read it aloud).

### Weekly structure
- **Mon:** Speaking — work roleplay (client meeting / contract negotiation).
- **Tue:** Listening + shadowing (one podcast episode).
- **Wed:** Vocabulary + chunks (review old ones + learn new collocations, drill with AI).
- **Thu:** Speaking — debate or "interview me" (freelance AI-agent job).
- **Fri:** Writing (LinkedIn post / project email) + read it aloud.
- **Sat:** Real human practice (language exchange app or conversation club) — the most important day.
- **Sun:** Light review + record a 2-minute talk to track progress. Rest is allowed.

### 90-day plan (B1 → solid B2 path)
- **Month 1 – Build the habit + confidence.** Short routine most days. Focus: roleplay + shadowing. Goal: speak daily without fear; one 2-minute recording per week. In week 1, take a free CEFR test + a vocabulary size test (your baseline).
- **Month 2 – Push complexity.** Add debates, 4/3/2, and chunks. Force longer answers. Add one real human session per week. Attack your top repeated errors.
- **Month 3 – Sound natural + professional.** Roleplay real work tasks (client presentation, contract). Ask AI for idioms and faster speech. Use less transcript help in listening. Re-test CEFR + vocabulary on day 90 and compare.

**What would change this plan:** If your day-90 CEFR test still says B1, you are probably not being *pushed* enough — add more explicit correction and harder tasks. If you feel burned out, shrink to the 15-minute version but keep the daily habit alive.

### Copy-paste prompts

**1) Roleplay tutor prompt**
> "You are my English conversation tutor. I am a B1 learner aiming for B2. Speak at clear B2 level. Play the role of [a client / interviewer]. Scenario: [describe]. Ask me one question at a time and keep the conversation going. Do NOT correct me during the talk. After 10 minutes, stop and give me: (1) my 3 most important mistakes with corrections, (2) 5 better phrases I could have used, (3) one thing I did well. Be honest, not too nice."

**2) Correction prompt**
> "Here is something I said/wrote: [text]. Correct it. Show: original → correction → short reason in simple words. Then rewrite it the way a fluent speaker would say it naturally (with idioms if useful)."

**3) Debate prompt**
> "Let's debate in English. Topic: [e.g. 'AI will change architecture jobs']. You take the opposite side. Push me with hard follow-up questions. Keep it at B2. At the end, list 3 grammar fixes and 5 useful debate phrases I should learn."

**4) Story-retelling prompt**
> "Tell me a short interesting story (about 1 minute) at B1–B2 level. Then ask me to retell it in my own words. Listen, then tell me what I missed and give me 5 phrases to make my retelling better."

**5) Vocabulary/chunks drill prompt**
> "Teach me 7 useful English collocations/chunks about [architecture / cosmology / freelancing]. For each: the phrase, a simple meaning, and one example sentence. Then quiz me: give me a gap-fill for each and make me use it in a sentence out loud."

**6) "Interview me" prompt**
> "Act as an interviewer for a freelance AI-agent developer role. Ask me 6 real interview questions, one at a time, and react like a real interviewer. At the end, rate my answers at CEFR level and give me stronger ways to say my key points."

**7) Pronunciation / shadowing prompt**
> "Give me 5 sentences about [topic] at natural speed. I will say each one back. Tell me which sounds or word stress I got wrong and how to fix them. Focus on the sounds Persian speakers of English usually find hard (like /w/ vs /v/, and 'th')."

### Creative sessions tied to his life
- **Architecture client meeting:** present a design concept and handle objections.
- **Explain a Revit/BIM workflow** to a non-technical client in simple English.
- **Negotiate a freelance contract** (price, deadline, scope) — roleplay.
- **Job interview** for AI-agent / 3D web visualization freelance work.
- **Cosmology talk:** explain a topic you love (black holes, the expanding universe) — great for complex language.
- **Describe a hiking trip:** past tenses and descriptive vocabulary.
- **LinkedIn post:** write it, get it corrected, then say it aloud as a 60-second video script.

### How to measure progress (objectively)
- **CEFR self-check grid** (Council of Europe self-assessment grid — free) once a month.
- **Free online CEFR placement tests** at start, day 45, and day 90.
- **Vocabulary size test** (free, ~8 minutes, e.g. vocabulary-test.com) to watch your word count grow.
- **Uninterrupted speech length:** record yourself and measure how many seconds/minutes you can talk without stopping, and count your long pauses. This should go up.
- **Words per minute** in your 4/3/2 talks (should rise, like in the research above).

## Solutions to Each AI Downside
- **Too agreeable / no correction →** Add to every prompt: "Correct all my important mistakes. Do not be too nice." Ask for a feedback report at the end of each session.
- **Fossilization →** Use delayed correction after each session; keep an error log; redo the same task until the error is gone.
- **Too formal / unnatural →** Ask the AI to "use casual English, idioms, and slang." Learn real phrases from podcasts and shows.
- **Too clear / slow / one accent →** Ask the AI to "speak faster and more casually." Use podcasts and real people for real, varied accents.
- **Over-reliance →** Keep a fixed weekly human session (Saturday). AI is your practice; humans are your real test.
- **Passive comprehension →** Force output first (try to speak before you get the input), and use pushed-output tasks like 4/3/2 and debates.
- **Hallucinated grammar →** Check any grammar rule against a trusted site (British Council LearnEnglish, Cambridge Dictionary).
- **Privacy →** Don't share private client or project data; use general or made-up examples in roleplays.

## Caveats
- The research is positive but **mixed**: some studies show no test-score gain, and most are short (6–8 weeks) with small groups. AI helps most when combined with real human practice.
- The "hours to B2" numbers are **guidelines**, not promises. Your speed depends mostly on daily consistency and being pushed.
- Many app reviews and "best of 2026" lists are published by the companies themselves, so treat exact prices and rankings as approximate — check the official app before you pay.
- AI speaking apps can misjudge your pronunciation because speech recognition is weaker for non-native accents; do not let a bad score discourage you.
